How this is calculated
💰
Cost to lease = money due at signing + all monthly payments over the term. You never build equity, and you hand the car back at the end.
📈
Cost to buy = down payment + loan payments made during your hold period, minus the equity you've built (resale value minus remaining loan balance) when you sell or trade in.
⚖
Leasing usually wins for shorter terms (2–3 years) since you avoid the steepest depreciation years. Buying usually wins if you keep the car 5+ years, since the loan gets paid off and the car still has value.

How it works

The lease side adds the money due at signing to every monthly payment over your time period. The buy side computes the loan payment from your price, down payment, APR, and term, then adds up the payments you make while you own the car. At the end, the car is worth either your resale estimate or, if you leave it blank, a value from our depreciation curve (20%, 15%, 13%, 12%, 10%, 9%, then 8% a year). Your equity is that value minus the loan balance still owed, and the net cost of buying is your down payment plus the payments made, minus that equity. The lower net cost wins.

Frequently asked questions

Does this include insurance, maintenance, and taxes?

No. Those costs are similar either way, so they are left out to keep the comparison simple. Leases can add fees, mileage overage charges, and excess wear charges that this tool does not model.

Why does the resale value matter so much?

It determines how much equity you walk away with when buying. A car that holds its value makes buying look better, and a fast-depreciating one makes leasing look better.

When does leasing usually make sense?

When you want a new car every few years, drive within the mileage limit, and prefer lower payments. Buying usually wins when you keep a car for five years or more.

What are typical lease mileage limits?

Many leases cap you at around 10,000 to 15,000 miles a year and charge for extra miles. Check the limit and the per-mile charge in your lease agreement.

Using the number

If the two totals are within a thousand dollars, you do not have a winner. You have a tie that will be broken by the mileage cap, the wear-and-tear bill, and whether you will want the car after the term. The lease side is the sum of due-at-signing and the payments. It does not add the 15 or 25 cents a mile a contract charges past the cap, and it does not add disposition fees. Type a resale value you believe. If you leave it blank, the page invents one from the same new-car curve as the depreciation calculator, which is a blunt instrument.

Buying wins more often when you keep the car well past the loan, because the equity line finally exceeds the extra payments. Leasing wins more often when you would replace the car at 36 months anyway and the payment is genuinely lower after a honest mileage estimate. Insurance and sales tax are not inside either total. Add them from the out-the-door page and your own quote before you tell yourself the chart picked a side.

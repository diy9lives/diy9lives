Understanding BMI
⚠
BMI has limitations. It does not account for muscle mass, bone density, or fat distribution. Athletes often show "overweight" BMI. Use it as a starting point, not a diagnosis.
💪
Waist circumference matters too. Health risk increases significantly for men with waist over 40 inches or women over 35 inches, regardless of BMI.
🏥
Talk to a doctor. BMI is a screening tool, not a medical diagnosis. A healthcare provider can give you a full picture of your health.

How it works

Body mass index is weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. With imperial units the tool uses the equivalent formula, 703 × weight in pounds ÷ height in inches squared. Standard adult categories are underweight below 18.5, normal from 18.5 to 24.9, overweight from 25 to 29.9, and obese at 30 and above (split into class I from 30 and class II from 35). The healthy range shown is the weight span that would put your BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 at your height.

Adult category cutoffs follow the ranges published by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention: underweight below 18.5, healthy weight from 18.5 to 24.9, overweight from 25.0 to 29.9, and obesity at 30.0 or above. The CDC splits obesity into class 1 (30.0 to 34.9), class 2 (35.0 to 39.9), and class 3 (40.0 or above). This tool labels 35.0 and above as class II+.

Frequently asked questions

Is BMI accurate for athletes?

Not always. BMI cannot tell muscle from fat, so very muscular people can land in the overweight range while having low body fat. Waist measurement and body composition give extra context.

Does BMI measure body fat?

No. It is a quick screening number based only on height and weight. A healthcare provider can use other measurements to get a fuller picture.

Can I use this for children?

No. This is the adult formula and categories. Children and teens are assessed with BMI-for-age percentiles, which a pediatrician can explain.

What should I do with my result?

Treat it as a starting point for a conversation with your doctor, not a diagnosis. Overall health depends on far more than one number.

Using the number

At 5 feet 10 inches and 185 pounds the index is about 26.5, which is the overweight band (25.0 to 29.9). The weight that would put this height between 18.5 and 24.9 is roughly 129 to 174 pounds. Eleven pounds is the gap to the top of that band. It is not an instruction to lose eleven pounds by Friday.

CDC’s adult example is built the same way. For someone 5 feet 9 inches, they list 169 to 202 pounds as overweight and 125 to 168 as the healthy-weight band. The categories are for adults 20 and older and they do not change with sex. The FAQ already covers athletes and children. The decision use of the number is whether to talk to a clinician and look at waist, blood pressure, and labs, not whether a single cutoff has diagnosed you. This page calls the middle band “normal.” CDC’s heading for the same range is “healthy weight.” The cutoffs match. The wording does not.

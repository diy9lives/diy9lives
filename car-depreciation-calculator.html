How it works

The calculator multiplies your price by a fixed year-by-year retention curve. For a new car the annual loss is 20%, 15%, 13%, 12%, 10%, 9%, then 8% a year, easing to 7% after year 10. A used car skips the steepest drop, starting at 15%, then 12%, 11%, 10%, 9%, 9%, and 8%. Each year's value becomes the base for the next, so the dollar loss shrinks as the car ages. You can project from 1 to 15 years.

This is a generic curve, not data for a specific make and model.

Frequently asked questions

Why do new cars lose value fastest in year one?

The first buyer pays for “new,” and that premium disappears the moment the car becomes used. After that, depreciation slows because the price is closer to what similar used cars sell for.

Which cars hold their value best?

Vehicles with strong reliability reputations and steady demand, such as certain trucks and Japanese brands, tend to depreciate more slowly than average. Luxury cars and some electric vehicles often lose value faster. This tool uses one average curve for all of them.

How do I find my car's actual value?

Check several valuation sites such as Kelley Blue Book, Edmunds, or CarGurus, and compare them with recent listings for similar cars near you. Mileage, condition, and options all matter.

Does mileage change the estimate?

Not in this tool. A car with much higher mileage than average will usually be worth less than the curve suggests, and one with low mileage may be worth more.

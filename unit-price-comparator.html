Finding the real cheapest package

  1. Enter each product with the same unit: ounces, pounds, count or litres. Mixing units is the most common way to get a wrong answer.
  2. Add the price and the package quantity. The page divides price by quantity and ranks the products.
  3. Read the unit price, not the sticker price. A $9.49 jar can be cheaper per ounce than a $4.29 jar.
  4. Check the shelf tag before you buy. Multi-buys, drained weights and loyalty prices can change the register total.

Match the units before dividing

Add one row per package you are comparing, then type the price as it appears on the shelf and the quantity in the package. The calculator divides price by quantity for every row and ranks them, so the cheapest unit price is the one worth buying. The important step is unit consistency: if one row is in ounces, make every row ounces. A 2-pound bag entered as "2" against a 32-ounce bag entered as "32" will look like a sixteen-fold bargain that does not exist.

For multi-buys, enter the total you actually hand over. "3 for $10" is a price of 10 and a quantity of 3. For a product sold by count — trash bags, diapers, tea bags — the quantity is the count, and the unit price becomes cost per bag rather than cost per ounce. Either way the ranking works.

The 24-ounce jar wins at 20.8 cents an ounce. The 16-ounce is 1 cent an ounce worse, which sounds trivial until you do the shopping-list version: 48 ounces costs $10.48 in 16-ounce jars and $9.98 in 24-ounce jars, a 50-cent difference on one item. The 48-ounce "family size" is the most expensive per ounce of the three, at 23.9 cents. That is the pattern worth watching for: big packaging is priced as convenience, not as a discount.

When the cheaper unit price is wrong

A unit price is a rate, and rates compare cleanly only when the thing being measured is the same. Two boxes of cereal at $0.31 and $0.28 per ounce are comparable; a box of cereal and a bag of flour are not, because you do not use an ounce of flour the way you use an ounce of cereal.

The second thing the number does not know is waste. A 48-ounce jar that goes stale half-finished has a real cost higher than 23.9 cents an ounce. If you use roughly 20 ounces a month, the 24-ounce jar is the right size and the 48-ounce is a slow-motion loss. The calculator tells you which package is cheapest per ounce; deciding whether you will finish it is the part that needs you.

Sales distort the ranking for exactly as long as the sale lasts. Re-enter the shelf price when the tag comes down, or the ranking you remember will be wrong on the next trip.

Unit price questions

Should I include sales tax?

You can skip it. Tax is a percentage of the price, so it scales every row by the same factor and never changes which one wins. It only matters if the items are taxed differently, which happens with food versus non-food in some states.

Why does the bigger package sometimes cost more per unit?

Because shelf space and the assumption that shoppers will not divide are both worth money. The number on the tag is a price, not a promise of value.

How do I compare a 2-pound bag against a 32-ounce bag?

Convert one to the other first. Sixteen ounces make a pound, so 2 pounds is 32 ounces and the two are directly comparable. Mixing pounds and ounces in the same comparison is the most common way to get a wrong answer.

Can I compare cost per use instead of cost per ounce?

Yes, and it is often better. For laundry detergent, enter the number of loads the bottle claims and the unit price becomes cost per load. Claims vary in honesty, so treat the bottle's number as the manufacturer's estimate.

What about "10 for $10" without buying ten?

Many stores charge the full unit price for a single item. If yours does, the honest comparison is one item at $1.29 against one item at $1.00 elsewhere; the 10-for-$10 price only exists if you take all ten.

Store labels and rounding

Unit price is price divided by quantity. NIST’s handbook material is the weights-and-measures context for how stores are supposed to state quantity.

  • NIST, unit conversion. Ounces, pounds, milliliters, and liters, so both packages are in one unit before you divide.

When unit price still misleads

The cheaper unit price is not the cheaper purchase if the extra product expires, needs storage, or replaces something you already own. The label may compare a drained weight with a net weight, or a multi-buy may not apply at the register. The calculator matches units; it cannot see the receipt, the shelf tag fine print or what you will actually use.

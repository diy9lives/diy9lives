What a car really costs you per year

The payment is the smallest number in the deal. Here is the rest of it.

By David Liu, founder of DIY9lives. Last reviewed November 26, 2026.

Every car conversation starts with the payment, and the payment is the one number that tells you almost nothing. A $480 lease and a $610 loan on the same vehicle can leave you $10,000 apart after three years. The only comparison that survives contact with reality is total cost per year of ownership, and it has four parts: what the car loses in value, what you pay in interest, what it costs to keep running, and what you get back when you sell it.

This is the order I use, and every figure below comes out of the calculators on this site, so you can reproduce it with your own numbers instead of trusting mine.

Depreciation: the bill that never arrives

Depreciation is the difference between what you paid and what the car is worth later, spread across the years you own it. Nobody invoices it, which is exactly why it gets ignored. It also happens to be the largest single line for most new cars.

The depreciation calculator applies a typical curve: 20% in year one, then 15%, 13%, 12%, and 10% for years two through five. On a $35,000 new car, that is $28,000 after one year, $23,800 after two, $20,706 after three, and $16,399 after five. The car has lost $18,601, or 53% of what you paid, and the average is about $3,720 a year.

The shape matters more than any single percentage. Year one alone is $7,000, and year five is about $1,800. That is why buying a two-year-old car skips the worst year of the curve, and why the cheapest miles you will drive are in a car you have already owned for four years. It is also why a five-year loan on a car that loses half its value in five years is a bet that you will keep it, because selling early turns the loss into a check you have to write.

The curve is a rule of thumb, not a quote for your model. Trucks and a few SUVs hold value better; luxury sedans and some EVs fall faster. Mileage and condition sit on top of age: two identical four-year-old cars can differ by thousands depending on 40,000 versus 90,000 miles. Before you believe any curve, look up actual asking prices for the year and mileage you are considering.

Interest: the price of not paying cash

Interest is easy to calculate and easy to bury in a longer term. A $26,680 loan at 6.9% for 60 months costs about $4,940 in interest; stretch it to 72 months and the payment drops by roughly $73 a month while the interest rises to about $5,980. You are buying a small monthly break for about $1,040, and you stay underwater longer.

The number to watch is the balance against the car's value, not the payment against your budget. If you owe $24,000 on a car worth $19,000, a total loss or a change of circumstances becomes a problem you cannot negotiate. The CFPB's auto-loan guide is the checklist for the add-ons and fees that never show up in the payment either.

Fuel: the line you can actually move

Fuel is the part of ownership you influence every week. The gas calculator is miles divided by MPG times price. At 1,200 miles a month, 28 MPG, and $3.49 a gallon, that is 42.9 gallons and $150 a month, or about $1,795 a year. The same driving at 42 MPG is $100 a month. Ten MPG is worth about $600 a year here, which is more than most people save by shopping around for gas.

Use your own MPG, measured from fill-ups, not the window sticker. Two tanks of gas and the odometer give you a real number, and it is usually several MPG worse than the label once you add hills, air conditioning, and a roof rack. EPA figures are the honest starting point for comparing two cars; they are not a prediction of your commute. The FuelEconomy.gov database is the same source dealers use, and it lists both the label value and the annual fuel estimate.

EV versus gas: the break-even is arithmetic, the decision is not

Comparing fuels is the cleanest calculation on the site and the most misused. The EV versus gas calculator at 12,000 miles a year: a 30 MPG car at $3.50 burns 400 gallons, or $1,400. An EV at 30 kWh per 100 miles uses 3,600 kWh. If 80% of that is home charging at $0.17 and 20% is public fast charging at $0.45, the annual electricity is about $814. The saving is $586 a year.

Against a $5,000 price premium, that is an 8.5-year payback on fuel alone. Drop the public charging share to zero and the same car pays back in about six and a half years. Push it to half and the case collapses. That is the whole argument in one variable, and it is why "EVs are cheaper to run" and "EVs are not worth it" can both be true depending on whether you can charge at home.

Public charging is a different rate for the same electrons, and the Department of Energy's electricity basics explain why. Maintenance usually favours the EV, but insurance, tyres, and depreciation often do not. Never decide on fuel alone; put both cars through the true cost calculator.

Lease versus buy: what the payment hides

A lease is a long rental with a fixed residual, and the payment only covers the part of the car you use. That is why it looks cheaper. Whether it is cheaper depends on what you would do with the car at the end.

The lease versus buy calculator, on a $38,000 SUV kept three years: $480 a month with $3,000 at signing is $20,280 out of pocket. Buying with $4,000 down at 6.5% over 60 months, then selling at $23,000 after three years, nets $19,883. Buying wins by about $400, and that is before the lease's mileage cap and wear charges, which are the two lines that turn a close call into a clear loss.

Leasing makes sense when you genuinely want a new car every three years, you drive within the mileage allowance, and you would rather not carry resale risk. It stops making sense when the down payment is large, the term is stretched to lower the payment, or you plan to keep the car. Those are the three ways a lease quietly costs more than the loan it replaced.

The whole stack on one car

A $34,000 sedan, $5,000 down, 6.5% for 60 months, kept five years, with $1,400 insurance, $900 maintenance, and $1,800 of fuel a year. The true cost calculator returns $8,723 a year, or about $727 a month, and $43,614 over five years.

Depreciation and interest together are more than half the cost, and neither one appears on a monthly statement. That is the argument for buying a car that is two or three years old, for putting more down if you can, and for keeping the car past the point where the curve flattens. It is also the argument against judging a deal by the payment: a $40 lower payment on a $3,000 cheaper car is not a saving if the car loses $2,000 more in value over the same three years.

A road trip is a good sanity check on all of this. The road trip calculator on 900 miles at 30 MPG and $3.50 puts fuel at $105 out of a $520 trip. Fuel is a fifth of the cost of going, and the hotel is nearly half. Per-mile ownership costs behave the same way: the driving is rarely the expensive part.

What I check before signing anything

  • Run the car through the depreciation curve at the price you are actually paying, not the sticker.
  • Put that annual loss into the true cost calculator with your real insurance quote and your measured MPG before you decide the payment is affordable.
  • Compare the lease and the loan over the same number of years, including what you would sell the car for at the end.
  • Get the out-the-door number in writing. Tax, title, and doc fees change the loan amount, and the loan amount is what you pay interest on.
  • Decode the VIN with the VIN decoder to confirm the car is the trim and engine you think you are buying. It will not tell you about accidents or mileage, so a history report is a separate check.

Where these numbers come from

Depreciation percentages are this site's rule of thumb, chosen to match typical new-car curves rather than any single study. Fuel and EV efficiency figures come from the EPA's public database. Loan math is standard amortisation. Where a number depends on your situation, the calculator asks you for it rather than guessing: your electricity rate, your insurance quote, your mileage.

  • FuelEconomy.gov. EPA fuel economy, annual fuel cost estimates, and EV efficiency in kWh per 100 miles.
  • Alternative Fuels Data Center. How residential and public charging rates differ.
  • CFPB auto loans. Fees, add-ons, and what to compare beyond the payment.
  • BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey. National averages for household vehicle spending, useful as a cross-check.

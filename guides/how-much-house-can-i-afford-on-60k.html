How much house
on $60,000

The salary sets a ceiling, not a target, and the taxes move the price.

By David Liu, founder of DIY9lives. Last reviewed November 9, 2026.

Salary is the input everyone starts with and the least useful one, because lenders do not lend against income. They lend against the payment, and the payment is a share of your monthly gross. This guide converts $60,000 into a monthly figure, then into a purchase price, and shows the three places the answer moves: the down payment, the property tax rate, and the other debts on your credit report.

From salary to a monthly ceiling

$60,000 a year is $5,000 a month before tax. Two ratios have been used by lenders for a long time. The first caps housing at 28% of gross, which is $1,400. The second caps total debt — housing plus car loans, student loans, minimum card payments — at 36%, which is $1,800.

Read them together and the second one is the constraint that actually bites. If housing takes the full $1,400, there is $400 a month of room for everything else. A $350 car payment and a $150 student loan already exceed it, so the house number has to come down.

These are guidelines rather than rules, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's home-buying material describes affordability as a wider question of income, debts, credit and savings. Plenty of borrowers are approved above 36%. Being approved is not the same as being comfortable, and this page is about the second thing.

The payment has four parts

A mortgage payment is principal, interest, property tax and homeowners insurance — PITI. Only the first two come from the loan; the last two come from the house, and they arrive every year whether or not the loan is paid off.

For budgeting, split the $1,400. A rough division is $1,100 for principal and interest and $300 for tax and insurance. That is only a starting guess: property tax in much of the country runs between half a percent and two percent of assessed value a year, and insurance varies by state, roof age and claims history.

Turning $1,100 into a price

At 6.75% over 30 years, a $1,100 monthly principal-and-interest payment supports a loan of about $169,600. The purchase price then depends on how much you put down, because the loan is the price minus the down payment.

The same monthly payment buys a house 19% more expensive at 20% down than at 5% down, because less of the payment is borrowed. That is the whole mechanism: the down payment is not just a hurdle to clear, it is what lets a given payment carry a bigger price.

On top of the down payment, budget for closing costs. They include lender fees, title work, appraisal, prepaid taxes and insurance, and they are typically a few percent of the price. The home affordability calculator takes them as an input rather than hiding them.

What $1,400 feels like after tax

A $60,000 salary does not arrive as $5,000. Federal income tax, Social Security and Medicare, and state tax take a share before the mortgage does. Depending on the state, take-home is often nearer $4,000 than $5,000, which makes a $1,400 payment roughly 35% of what actually lands in the account.

That gap is why the 28% guideline feels tighter than it sounds. It is measured against gross income, but every other bill in your life is paid from net. Running the numbers through the salary calculator and the mortgage calculator before house-hunting gives you the two figures that matter: what you take home, and what the payment costs.

Where the number goes wrong

Property tax

The same $212,000 house costs different amounts in two towns a mile apart. At 0.6% a year the tax is about $106 a month; at 2% it is about $353. That $247 difference supports roughly $38,000 less loan at 6.75%, which is a large chunk of house for a line item most buyers never check before the offer.

Insurance

Homeowners insurance has been rising in many markets, and it is priced on rebuild cost rather than purchase price. A coastal property, an older roof or a history of claims can put the annual premium well above the national habit. Get a real quote for the address before you decide the payment is comfortable.

Mortgage insurance and HOA dues

Below 20% down, conventional loans usually carry mortgage insurance until the balance falls enough; FHA loans have their own rules. A condominium or a house in a subdivision with a homeowners association adds a monthly dues figure that lenders count in your debt ratio but that never buys you equity.

The three numbers to walk in with

  1. A payment ceiling from your budget, not from a lender. Work out what you can pay every month while still saving and still handling a repair, then treat that as the limit.
  2. A reserve. A house produces bills that arrive without warning: a water heater, a roof, a furnace. A cash reserve of a few months of payments is part of affordability, not a separate goal.
  3. A price ceiling from the ratios. Then subtract the tax and insurance you actually expect, and see whether the two numbers agree. When they disagree, the lower one is the honest answer.

For $60,000 with modest other debt, that usually lands somewhere in the $170,000 to $210,000 range depending on the down payment and the local tax rate. The paid-off mortgage guide's sibling question — rent or buy — is worth running too, because in expensive markets the break-even year can be long enough that renting and investing the difference wins.

What the ratios do not see

A debt-to-income calculation has no line for childcare, commuting, or the cost of a second car in a place without transit. Two households on $60,000 can have the same ratio and very different amounts left after the essentials. Before accepting a lender's maximum, write down the fixed monthly costs that will not change when you move — childcare, loan payments, insurance, phone, transport — and subtract them from take-home pay. What remains is the honest housing number, and it is usually below the ratio.

HOA dues deserve particular suspicion. They are counted in the debt ratio, so a lender will see them, but they are also the one housing cost that can rise without you being able to do anything about it. A $250 monthly assessment is $3,000 a year that buys no equity; over ten years it is the price of a kitchen.

The other thing the ratios ignore is the shape of your income. A $60,000 salary is steady; $60,000 of freelance income is not, and a lender will average it over two years for exactly that reason. If the income is variable, the payment ceiling should be built on the worst recent year rather than the best.

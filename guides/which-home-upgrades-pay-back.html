Which home upgrades actually pay back

Almost none of them break even. The useful question is which ones lose the least, and what the loss buys you while you live there.

By David Liu, founder of DIY9lives. Last reviewed November 26, 2026.

Every renovation conversation reaches the same question: will I get this money back? The honest answer, for nearly every project, is no. The renovation ROI calculator works from national average return rates, and the highest of them still returns 91 cents on the dollar. A project that recovers most of its cost is a good project. A project that recovers all of it is rare, and usually it is a repair rather than an upgrade.

That does not make renovating pointless. It makes the comparison the point. Two projects can cost the same and leave you in very different positions three years later, and the difference is visible before you pick up a phone.

Read the last column as the price of the upgrade, not the cost of it. A garage door costs $2,000 and charges you $180 to own. A master suite costs $60,000 and charges you $22,200. Both are fine decisions if you want the thing, but only one of them is close to free.

Why the cheap exterior projects win

Garage doors, entry doors and roofs return the most because they are the cheapest way to change how a house is judged from the kerb. A buyer decides in the first thirty seconds whether a house feels cared for, and that judgement is made from the street, from the photos, and from the first step inside the front door. Nothing you do in the back of the house competes with that.

There is also an arithmetic reason. The return rates are percentages, so a small project that recovers 91% loses very little in absolute money, while a large project that recovers 74% loses a lot. That is not an argument against the kitchen. It is an argument for doing the cheap visible work first, because it protects the value of everything behind it. A beautiful kitchen in a house with a peeling garage door is a kitchen that sells for less than it should.

Roofs sit in a different category. A roof at the end of its life is not an upgrade, it is a defect, and buyers price defects harshly because they are easy to see and expensive to fix. Replacing a worn roof is closer to maintenance than renovation, which is why it holds value better than a cosmetic project of the same cost.

The kitchen is not the best investment, it is the most visible one

Kitchens and bathrooms dominate renovation talk because they are what people remember from a viewing. The mid-range kitchen returns 74%, which is good, and a major kitchen returns 58%, which is not. The gap between those two numbers is the most useful thing on this page.

The difference is not quality, it is fit. A mid-range kitchen brings a tired room up to the standard of the house. A major kitchen, with the wall removed and the appliances upgraded two tiers, can push a house past what its street supports. When a $400,000 house gets a $75,000 kitchen, the finished house is not worth $475,000; it is worth whatever the best house on the block is worth, and the owner has spent the difference on a room they are about to leave.

The same logic explains why a bathroom addition (56%) returns less than a bathroom remodel (70%). Adding a bathroom changes the category of the house, which is valuable, but it also adds square footage, plumbing and a permanent maintenance obligation, and the market does not pay for all three at once.

What a return rate leaves out

A return rate is a resale number. It says nothing about the years you spend in the house before the sale, and those years are most of the value for most people.

Divide the net loss by the years you expect to stay and the numbers become comparable to a monthly bill. The $25,000 kitchen loses $6,500, which is $1,300 a year over five years, or $650 a year over ten. A new kitchen for $54 a month is a different proposition from a $6,500 mistake, and it is the same project. The same sum applied to the master suite gives $4,440 a year over five years, which is a real commitment rather than a rounding error.

The second thing the percentage hides is the overrun. Return rates apply to what you actually spend, so a project that runs 20% over budget does not recover 20% more value; the extra comes straight out of your pocket and the net loss grows by the full amount. A $25,000 kitchen that lands at $32,000 turns a $6,500 loss into a $14,000 one. Contingency is not pessimism, it is part of the cost.

Third, condition. Two kitchens at the same price are not the same project if one replaces a functioning 1990s kitchen and the other replaces a kitchen with a leaking dishwasher and no extractor. Fixing a defect protects value that already exists; the rate was never designed to capture that.

The neighbourhood ceiling

Every street has a price that buyers will not exceed for that location, school and lot. Above it, money stops converting into value, and the conversion gets worse the further past the ceiling you go. This is the single most common way a well-intentioned renovation loses money, and no calculator can tell you where the line is.

The test is simple. Look at what the best-kept houses on your street sold for in the last year. If your finished project would put your house above that number, expect to recover a smaller share than the table says. If your house is already the weakest on the street, the same project can return more, because it is closing a gap rather than opening one.

An agent who sells on your street knows this number in a way a national average cannot. That is what the custom field in the calculator is for.

A decision order for a real house

  1. Fix what is broken first. Roof, gutters, drainage, heating, anything a home inspector would flag. These are not investments, they are the price of a house that can be sold at all, and they protect every dollar spent later.
  2. Do the cheap visible work. A garage door, a front door, exterior paint and tidy landscaping. The highest return per dollar on this page lives here, and the work is fast.
  3. Bring the kitchen or bathroom up to the standard of the house, not above it. Mid-range, well executed, neutral. This is where the 70% and 74% rates come from, and where over-improving starts.
  4. Only then consider an addition. Square footage is the most expensive way to add value and the least reversible. Do it because you need the room, and treat the resale figure as a partial recovery rather than a plan.
  5. Set a contingency before you start. Ten to twenty per cent on anything that opens a wall. It is the difference between an overrun you can absorb and one that stops the job halfway.

What I would not do for resale

Anything highly personal: the bold tile, the wall of built-in shelving for your specific collection, the garage converted into a gym. These can be excellent decisions for the years you live there and poor ones at sale, because the next owner has to undo them. The same applies to a pool in a market where pools are not expected, and to a sunroom in a climate where it is usable for eight weeks a year. The rate for a sunroom is 55%, and that is an average across the whole country; in the wrong market it is worse.

If the goal is purely financial, the best renovation is often the one you do not do. Paint, repair, declutter and sell is a strategy with almost no downside and no overrun. Everything else on this page is a decision about how you want to live, with a resale figure attached.

Where these numbers come from

The percentages are the planning factors built into this site's renovation ROI calculator, chosen to sit in the range that national cost-versus-value studies report. They are averages by project type, not an appraisal of your house. The typical costs in the table are round national figures for the size of job described.

  • CFPB, Owning a home. What lenders and appraisers look at, which is often the work that protects value rather than adds it.
  • Census, American Housing Survey. National data on housing condition, age and improvement spending.
  • ENERGY STAR building products. Windows, doors and roofing, and what the efficiency claims actually cover.
  • National Association of Home Builders. Remodeling market data and what is being built where.

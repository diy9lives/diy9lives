How much paint
for one room

Wall area, not floor area — and the two coats nobody budgets for.

By David Liu, founder of DIY9lives. Last reviewed November 9, 2026.

Most people buy paint by looking at a room and guessing. The guess is usually low, because the thing you are painting is the walls, and the walls are bigger than the floor. A 12 by 10 foot room has 120 square feet of floor and roughly four times that much wall. This guide walks the arithmetic that the paint calculator runs, so you can check its answer against your own measurements before you drive to the store.

Start with wall area, not floor area

Wall area is the perimeter of the room multiplied by the height of the ceiling. A 12 by 10 foot room has a perimeter of 12 + 10 + 12 + 10, which is 44 feet. With 9 foot walls that is 396 square feet of wall — more than three times the floor.

Measure the perimeter yourself rather than trusting the room's name. A "12 by 10" bedroom with a closet bump-out and a hallway alcove is not 44 feet around, and those two features can add 60 square feet of wall. Measure each wall separately and add them; it takes two minutes and saves a trip.

Ceiling height is the other input people round. An older house with 8 foot ceilings has 11% less wall than the same room at 9 feet, which is often the difference between two gallons and three. Measure floor to ceiling in one corner, not in the middle where the ceiling may be higher.

Subtract the doors and windows

You do not paint the glass, so the openings come out of the total. The calculator uses 20 square feet for a standard door and 15 for a window. Those are averages for common openings, not code: a 36 by 80 inch door is exactly 20 square feet, while a standard double-hung window is closer to 12.

For a 12 by 10 room with one door and two windows the subtraction is 20 + 15 + 15, so 50 square feet. That takes 396 down to 346 square feet of paintable wall.

Do not subtract a door you intend to paint. If the door, its casing and the baseboard are getting the same colour, they are wall area now, and the trim will use more paint per square foot than a flat wall does because of the edges and the profile.

Two coats is the number that matters

A single coat over a similar colour can work. A single coat over a dark colour, a patch, or bare drywall will not, and the second coat is not optional in practice. Budget for two, and treat one coat as a bonus you occasionally get.

The buffer exists because a roller leaves paint in the tray, a cutting-in brush drinks more than you expect, and touching up a scuff in six months needs the same batch. Ten percent is enough for a room; fifteen is safer if you are spraying, where overspray and masking waste more.

Add the ceiling and the order changes. A 12 by 10 ceiling is 120 square feet, which at two coats is 240 square feet, or 0.60 gallons, or 0.66 with the buffer. That pushes the total from 1.90 to 2.56 gallons, and 2.56 rounds up to 3 gallons. This is the most common surprise in the whole exercise: painting the ceiling costs a whole extra gallon, not a splash.

What a gallon actually covers

Paint labels state coverage, typically somewhere between 350 and 450 square feet per gallon for interior wall paint. The calculator offers 350, 400 and 450 as presets. The number on the can wins, and it is stated for a smooth, primed, non-porous surface.

Three things move you toward the low end. New drywall absorbs paint, so the first coat covers less than the second. A dramatic colour change needs more coats, not more paint per coat. And a highly textured wall has more surface area than its flat dimensions suggest — a heavy orange-peel texture can be 20% more actual surface than the measurement implies.

Primer is separate and is not a coat of paint. Bare drywall, patched spots, and any repair that shows through need a primer, and tinted primer toward your topcoat colour can save a coat on a dark-to-light change. It is cheaper per gallon than paint and does the sealing job better.

Trim, doors and the other paint

Trim is measured in linear feet, not square feet, and a gallon goes a long way: a quart usually handles the trim for one room. A door slab takes roughly 20 square feet per side, so a two-sided door plus its casing is about a quart on its own if you are brushing it.

Buy trim paint in the same sheen family you want, and do not try to stretch wall paint onto trim. Wall paint is formulated for scrubbability at low sheen or for a flat finish that hides imperfections; trim wants an enamel that resists sticking and cleans up.

Buying the right amount

Buy all of the paint for one room at once, and check the batch codes on the cans. Two cans from different batches can differ slightly, and the difference shows on a large flat wall in raking light, especially with a light colour. If you must buy a second can later, keep a little of the first for touch-ups.

Do not buy extra "for next time" unless you have somewhere to store it. Paint does not keep indefinitely once opened, and a half-full can left in a garage through a freeze-and-thaw cycle is a can you will not trust on a wall.

Before any of this: how old is the paint?

If the house was built before 1978, assume the existing paint may contain lead until you know otherwise. Sanding, scraping or heat-stripping that paint is a different kind of job with different rules, and the EPA's renovation, repair and painting program is where to start reading. Encapsulating with a fresh coat over intact paint is a different situation from disturbing it, and the distinction matters for anyone in the house, especially children.

How the answer changes with the room

The same method gives very different orders depending on the shape of the space. A long narrow room has more wall per square foot of floor than a square one, so a 10 by 16 has 52 feet of perimeter against the 12 by 10's 44, and 29% more paint for the same floor area. A room that is mostly glass — a sunroom or a converted porch — has large openings to subtract, and the subtraction can be more than a fifth of the total.

Closets are separate decisions. A bedroom closet is usually painted the same colour as the room, and its interior walls are another 40 to 60 square feet for a walk-in and around 20 for a reach-in. If it is getting the same colour, add it; if the closet is staying as it is, the door is already in your subtraction and there is nothing more to do.

Open-plan spaces break the perimeter method, because there is no fourth wall. Treat the space as its own room: add the length of every wall you will actually paint, including the short return walls at either end of an opening, and multiply by height. Where a ceiling changes height, split the wall into two bands and add them. It is the same arithmetic, just counted in pieces.

Cabinets and trim you are keeping

Cabinets subtract nothing and complicate everything. A kitchen wall behind the units is painted once, roughly, and then hidden. Paint it, because the next owner will remove the cabinets, but do not agonise over the finish. Wall area behind built-in shelving is the same story.

Trim is worth thinking about before you buy wall paint, because the colour decision usually runs the other way: many rooms look right with the walls in a flat finish and the trim in a semi-gloss of the same colour, which means one gallon of paint can tint both if you ask for two sheens of the same base.

For everything else, the arithmetic is the whole job: perimeter times height, minus the openings, times the number of coats, divided by the coverage on the can, plus a buffer. The room above needs two gallons for the walls and three if the ceiling is included, and no amount of careful rolling changes that.

Guide

Why the paycheck
is smaller

Six deductions, in the order they come off, on a $60,000 salary.

By David Liu, founder of DIY9lives. Last reviewed November 9, 2026.

A salary is an annual gross figure, and a paycheck is a net figure after six different things have been taken out. The gap is not a mistake and it is not all tax. This guide walks a $60,000 salary down to a monthly deposit and names each subtraction as it happens, using the same categories as the salary calculator.

The starting point

$60,000 a year is $5,000 a month gross, or about $2,308 per two-week pay period. Everything below is a percentage or a bracket applied to that number.

Deduction one: pre-tax benefits

Before any tax is calculated, payroll subtracts the things you elected: your share of health insurance premiums, dental and vision, a health savings account contribution, a traditional 401(k) deferral, and any dependent care or commuter benefit. These come off the top and reduce taxable wages, which is why they are the cheapest deductions available.

A 5% 401(k) contribution on $60,000 is $3,000 a year, or $250 a month, and it comes out before income tax. If the employer matches part of it, that match is additional compensation that never appears in your salary figure.

Deduction two: Social Security

6.2% of gross wages, up to an annual wage base. That base is $184,500 for 2026, according to the Social Security Administration, so a $60,000 salary is nowhere near the ceiling and the full 6.2% applies. On $5,000 a month that is $310.

This is not optional and it is not adjusted by your tax withholding choices. Above the wage base the deduction stops for the year, which is why a high earner's December paycheck looks different from their January one.

Deduction three: Medicare

1.45% of gross wages, with no ceiling. On $5,000 that is $72.50. An additional 0.9% applies to wages above a threshold that depends on filing status, which most $60,000 salaries do not reach.

Social Security and Medicare together are 7.65%, and they are the deductions people most often mistake for income tax. They are not: they are insurance contributions, and they are calculated on gross wages before the standard deduction exists.

Deduction four: federal income tax

This is the bracket calculation, applied to taxable wages. Take gross, subtract pre-tax benefits, subtract the standard deduction, then run the remainder through the marginal brackets. The 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 for a single filer and $32,200 for married filing jointly, per IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32.

For a single filer with no pre-tax deductions, taxable income is $60,000 minus $16,100, which is $43,900. The tax on that is about $5,020 for the year, or roughly $418 a month. Note how much lower that is than the 22% bracket people quote: the bracket applies to the last dollars, not to all of them. The effective rate here is about 8.4% of gross.

What payroll withholds is an estimate based on the W-4 you filed. Claim more allowances or dependants and less is withheld, which feels like a raise and is really a smaller refund. The IRS Publication 15-T is the document that governs the withholding tables.

Deduction five: state and local tax

Nine states have no wage income tax, and the rest range from low single digits to double digits. A 3% state rate on $5,000 is $150 a month; a 6% rate is $300. Some cities add their own payroll or resident tax on top, which is a separate line on the stub and a separate return in April.

This is the single biggest source of variation in take-home between two people on the same salary, and it is why a national "average" take-home figure is close to meaningless.

Deduction six: post-tax deductions

Anything subtracted after tax: a Roth 401(k) contribution, a wage garnishment, a union dues payment, a charitable deduction through payroll, or a repayment of a salary advance. These do not reduce taxable income, so they cost more per dollar than the pre-tax items above.

Add a 5% 401(k) deferral and a $150 payroll health premium and the deposit falls to roughly $3,600, while the federal tax line also drops a little because taxable wages fell. The contributions did not vanish; they went into the account.

Why the first paycheck of the year looks different

Payroll systems recalculate withholding as the year progresses, and some benefits reset: a health savings account contribution may restart, a deductible may reset, and the Social Security wage base resets in January. A 27-paycheck year — which happens to anyone paid biweekly — also produces two months with a third check, which is why a budget built on "two paychecks a month" runs short in those months and long in the others.

The useful habit is to budget on the net figure, not the salary. Take the number that actually arrives, subtract fixed costs, and treat the salary as a headline that describes your negotiating position rather than your spending capacity.

How to read a pay stub

Every stub has three zones. The top is the pay period and the hours or salary basis. The middle is the current period, line by line. The bottom is year-to-date, and that is the column to check when something looks wrong, because one odd paycheck is usually a timing issue while a wrong year-to-date figure is a real error.

Compare the year-to-date gross against the salary divided by the number of pay periods so far. A mismatch of one period is normal in the first month of a job. A mismatch of several hundred dollars is worth a question to payroll, and the earlier you ask, the easier it is to fix.

Check the tax lines against the W-4 you filed. If you claimed dependants or extra withholding, the federal line should reflect it. If it does not, the form may not have been processed, and the correction is easier in February than in December.

Keep the last stub of the year. It is the record of what was withheld, and it is what you will need if a W-2 does not match your own arithmetic in the spring. Most payroll systems keep them, but a downloaded copy is worth having.

Overtime, bonuses and the flat-rate surprise

Supplemental pay — bonuses, commissions and overtime premiums — is often withheld at a flat 22% federal rate rather than through the bracket tables, because payroll systems treat it separately from regular wages. That is a withholding method, not a tax rate, and it is why a bonus sometimes produces a smaller deposit than the arithmetic in your head suggested.

Withholding is an estimate. If 22% turns out to be more than you owe on that money, the difference returns as a larger refund; if it is less, the gap is settled in April. Neither outcome changes the tax, only the timing.

Overtime has a second layer. The premium portion is still wages, so Social Security and Medicare apply, and a large single check can push the withholding band for that period. The year-to-date column matters more than the current one for that reason.

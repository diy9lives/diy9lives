How it works

Each month the balance grows by the monthly equivalent of your chosen compounding frequency, (1 + annual rate ÷ n)n/12 − 1, and then your monthly contribution is added at the end of the month. The year-by-year table shows the balance, total contributed, and interest earned. Changing the compounding frequency changes only how often interest is added, not your contributions.

The result is a projection with a constant rate, with no taxes, fees, or inflation.

Frequently asked questions

How much does compounding frequency matter?

Less than most people expect. For $10,000 at 7% over 10 years, annual compounding gives about $19,672 and daily compounding about $20,136. The rate and the time horizon matter far more.

What is the Rule of 72?

Divide 72 by your annual return to estimate how many years it takes to double your money. At 8%, that is about nine years.

Does this account for taxes, fees, or inflation?

No. Real returns are lower after investment fees, taxes on gains, and inflation, and actual returns vary from year to year.

What return rate should I use?

Nobody can promise a future return. Many planners use conservative long-term estimates and test several rates, such as a low, middle, and high case, to see a range.

Using the number

The defaults on this page are $5,000 to start, $200 added at the end of each month, 7% a year, compounded monthly, for 10 years. You put in $29,000. The balance is about $44,665. The other $15,665 is growth. That is the number to argue with, not the formula. If you change only the rate to 5%, the balance falls by several thousand dollars. If you change only the years to 20, it more than doubles the story, because the last decade grows the first decade’s balance.

A 7% type-in is a nominal guess. This page does not subtract inflation, fees, or taxes. The retirement calculator does the inflation step separately, which is why you should not also type a “real” 7% there if you already lowered the rate for inflation. The Rule of 72 said on the page (72 ÷ 7 is about 10 years to double) describes the $5,000 lump, not the $200 deposits. The deposits have less time, so they do not all double.

Use it to see whether a monthly transfer is doing more work than the starting balance. In this example the deposits are $24,000 of the $29,000 you put in. The starting $5,000 is not the hero.

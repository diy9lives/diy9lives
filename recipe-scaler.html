Scaling a recipe without guessing



  1. Paste the ingredients one per line, including the amount and unit. The parser understands fractions, decimals and common units.
  2. Set the original servings and the servings you want. A recipe for four scaled to eight doubles every ingredient.
  3. Read the scaled list and the pan note. If the batter gets deeper, the pan area did not double, so the time and temperature need attention.
  4. Taste and adjust salt, spice and leavening last. Those rarely scale linearly, especially in baking.




Original yield, new yield and pan size


Paste your ingredients one per line, exactly as the recipe writes them, then choose the multiplier or set the servings you want. The scaler reads the number at the start of each line, multiplies it, and prints the result as a fraction where that reads better than a decimal. Lines with no leading number pass through unchanged.


Write fractions the way a recipe does: 1/2 cup, 1 1/2 tsp. The parser handles both, and it keeps the unit and the ingredient text so the list stays readable.

What scales and what does not


Linear scaling is right for almost everything and wrong for a few things. Salt, leavening and strong spices usually scale with the batch, but a double batch of a braise often does not need double the salt, because the liquid evaporates from the same surface area and concentrates differently. Chilli and garlic are matters of taste rather than chemistry.


Pan size and cooking time do not scale at all. Doubling a cake recipe and pouring it into the same tin produces a burnt edge and a raw middle; the batter needs a larger tin, and the time changes by less than double because heat reaches the centre through a shorter path relative to volume. For anything baked in a fixed tin, scale the recipe to fit a tin you own rather than the other way round.


Eggs are the honest limit of fraction arithmetic. One and a half eggs is measurable; one and a quarter is a judgement call. For custards and pastry, weigh the beaten egg and take the percentage you need rather than trying to halve a yolk.



Recipe scaling questions


Can it read metric and imperial units?
Yes, the unit is passed through as text. It does not convert between them, so 250 g stays 250 g and does not become ounces.


What if my ingredients are in one paragraph?
Put each on its own line. The tool works line by line, and a paragraph becomes a single unreadable line.


Why did a line come back unchanged?
It had no number at the start. Instructions and headings pass through untouched, which is intended.


Should I scale the cooking time too?
No. Time depends on thickness and pan, not on quantity. Check earlier and test rather than multiplying minutes.


How do I halve a recipe with three eggs?
Beat them, weigh the mixture, and use half by weight. One and a half eggs is about three tablespoons.

What scaling cannot fix


Salt, leavening, spice and strong aromatics rarely scale linearly. Pan area, not volume, decides whether a doubled batter fits; a deeper pan needs a lower temperature and a longer time. The calculator does not know your oven, altitude or ingredient substitutions. For baking, weigh the ingredients and change one variable at a time.

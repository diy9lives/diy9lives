How to use this calculator

Room air conditioners are sized in BTU per hour, and the rating on the box is a capacity, so the goal is to match capacity to the heat your room actually gains.

  1. Enter the floor area in square feet. For an L-shaped room, measure each rectangle and add them.
  2. Enter the ceiling height. Eight feet is the baseline; anything taller is scaled by height divided by 8, because more air volume means more to cool.
  3. Set the sun exposure. A west or south wall of glass that bakes all afternoon is the sunny setting; a north-facing room under trees is shaded.
  4. Count the people who are normally in the room. Each person past two adds 600 BTU.
  5. Add the kitchen allowance if the unit cools a kitchen, because a stove and a refrigerator are heat sources the square footage never sees.

The result is rounded to the nearest 100 BTU, then matched up to the closest standard size.

A worked example: a sunny 240 square foot bedroom

Start at 20 BTU per square foot: 240 × 20 is 4,800 BTU. The ceiling is 10 feet, so the height scaling multiplies by 10 ÷ 8, which is 1.25, and the number is 6,000 BTU.

The room faces west and gets direct afternoon sun, so the sunny factor of 1.1 takes it to 6,600. Three people are usually in it, so one person past two adds 600, bringing it to 7,200. No kitchen adjustment. The page rounds to 7,200 BTU and recommends the smallest standard size at or above it, which is 8,000 BTU.

Notice what happened: the plain square-foot rule alone would have picked 5,000 or 6,000 BTU, and that unit would run continuously on a July afternoon without ever quite getting there. The same room with an 8 foot ceiling, no sun, and one person would come out at 4,800 and take the 5,000 BTU unit. The extra 3,000 BTU of capacity is bought entirely by ceiling height, glass, and bodies, not by floor area.

Add a kitchen to this same room and the 4,000 BTU allowance pushes it to 11,200, which lands on a 12,000 BTU unit.

What your results mean

The recommendation is a step on a ladder, and the ladder is not continuous: 5,000, 6,000, 8,000, 10,000, 12,000, 14,000, 15,000, 18,000, 24,000, 30,000, 36,000, 48,000, and 60,000 BTU. You buy the step above your number because a unit that is slightly too small never shuts off.

Slightly too big is a different problem. An oversized air conditioner blasts cold air, satisfies the thermostat quickly, and switches off before it has pulled much humidity out of the room. The result is a room that feels cold and clammy at the same time, and the fix is a smaller unit, not a lower setting.

Tons are a contractor's unit, not a weight: one ton is 12,000 BTU per hour. The page shows the figure so you can follow an HVAC quote, which will be written in tons.

This is a single-room rule of thumb, and it assumes a normal house. Ductless mini-splits, whole-house systems, and rooms with unusual glass area or no insulation need a proper load calculation, which accounts for wall construction, window performance, and local climate rather than square footage.

Frequently asked questions

Is a bigger air conditioner better?

No. Oversizing trades humidity control for speed. You want the smallest unit that holds the room at your setpoint on the hottest day, which is what the step-above-your-number rule approximates.

How many BTU for a 300 square foot room?

At 8 foot ceilings and two people, 20 BTU per square foot gives 6,000. With a 9 foot ceiling that becomes 6,750, so 7,000 and a 8,000 BTU unit. Sunny and third occupant add more on top.

Does ceiling height really matter that much?

Yes, because you are cooling a volume, not a footprint. Ten foot ceilings are 25% more air than eight foot ceilings over the same floor, and vaulted rooms are worse still.

What about a window unit versus a portable?

The BTU rating is comparable but the real-world capacity is not. A portable unit dumps some of its own heat back into the room through its exhaust, so a portable often needs a size up from the same window rating.

Will this work for heating?

No. It estimates cooling load only. Heating depends on the climate, the insulation, and the equipment, and a heat pump is sized from a different calculation.

Why does the page stop at 60,000 BTU?

Because that is the practical ceiling for a single room unit. Beyond it you are sizing a central system or multiple heads, and the honest answer is a professional load calculation rather than a rule of thumb.

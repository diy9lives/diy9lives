How to use this calculator

Bulk material is ordered by volume and priced by the yard, the bag, or the ton depending on where you buy it. Get the volume right first and the pricing conversation gets short.

  1. Measure the bed or path. For a rectangular bed, length times width. For a curved bed, break it into rectangles and add them, or measure the total area and type it in directly.
  2. Pick the material, which sets a sensible starting depth: 2 to 3 inches of mulch, about 4 inches of topsoil for new lawn, 2 to 3 inches of gravel for a path.
  3. Set the extra percentage. Five percent is the page's default because bags and bulk loads settle, and beds are never quite rectangular.
  4. Read the cubic yards for a bulk order and the bag count for a store run. Both describe the same pile.
  5. Use the weight figure only as a planning number. It is what turns a yard into a scale ticket if you are buying by the ton.

A worked example: three beds along a fence

Three beds: 24 by 4 is 96 square feet, 16 by 5 is 80, and 10 by 3 is 30. The total is 206 square feet. At 2.5 inches deep that is 206 × 2.5 ÷ 12, or 42.9 cubic feet.

Divided by 27, that is 1.59 cubic yards. In 2 cubic foot bags it is 22 bags. At the page's planning weight of about 800 pounds per yard, the pile weighs roughly 1,272 pounds, a bit over half a ton.

The decision the numbers point at: 22 bags is a cart and two trips, and one bulk yard is 1 cubic yard, so you would order 2 yards to cover 1.59 and have a little left. Bulk is the cheaper way to buy this volume almost anywhere, and it is only the right answer if a truck can get within wheelbarrow distance of the beds. If the beds are behind a gate at the back of a lot, the bags win on effort even though they lose on price.

What your results mean

Cubic yards are how landscape suppliers sell. Cubic feet are how bags are labeled. One yard is 27 cubic feet, or 13.5 of the 2 cubic foot bags, and that conversion is the only thing standing between a sensible order and three trips to the store.

Depth is the variable that changes the answer most, and it is the one people guess. Two inches of mulch suppresses weeds and holds moisture; four inches smothers plants and wastes material against the trunk. Keep mulch a few inches away from stems and siding rather than piling it against them.

The weight figures are planning averages, not measurements of your pile. Moisture moves them a lot: dry mulch and a load that sat in the rain are different weights, and compost is heavier than either. If you are buying by the ton, ask the yard what their material weighs rather than trusting the default.

Gravel is a different material with a different density, which is why the page carries separate numbers. Using the mulch weight for a gravel driveway understates the load by more than three times, and gravel is usually sold by the ton precisely because the volume-to-weight relationship matters that much.

Frequently asked questions

How deep should mulch be?

Two to three inches for most beds. Less does not suppress weeds; more holds too much moisture against stems. Refresh by topping up the faded layer rather than adding three fresh inches every year.

How many bags is a yard of mulch?

Thirteen and a half 2 cubic foot bags, so buy 14. A 3 cubic foot bag is 9 to a yard. Bag sizes vary by brand and region, which is why the calculator asks for the bag size you are actually buying.

How much does a yard of mulch weigh?

This page uses about 800 pounds as a planning figure. Wet mulch is considerably heavier, and a yard of gravel at about 2,800 pounds is a different order entirely. Ask the supplier for their number if you are paying by weight.

Is bulk cheaper than bags?

Almost always, once you are past a yard, and often even below that. The catch is delivery, a minimum order, and access for the truck. Compare the delivered price against the bag price before deciding.

How do I measure a curved bed?

Approximate it with rectangles, or measure the average width and multiply by the length along the curve. Then type the total square footage into the area box and skip the length and width fields.

How much topsoil for new grass?

About 4 inches over the seed area, which is 1 cubic yard per 81 square feet. For overseeding an existing lawn, a quarter inch is enough and the quantity drops by a factor of sixteen.

How it works

Two widely used formulas estimate your one-rep max from a lighter set. Epley is weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30), and Brzycki is weight × 36 ÷ (37 − reps). The tool averages them, with reps limited to 1 through 15. The training table then shows common percentages of that estimate, such as 85% for heavy strength sets or 70% for higher-rep muscle-building work.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is a one-rep max estimate?

It is most reliable for sets of about 10 reps or fewer. The more reps you use, the less accurate it gets, and form, fatigue, and the exercise itself all affect it.

Is it safe to test a true one-rep max?

It carries more injury risk than lighter work. If you test one, warm up well, use proper form, and have a spotter or safety bars. Estimates are often enough for programming.

Which formula should I trust?

Both are reasonable and give similar numbers at low reps, which is why the tool averages them. Neither is exact for every lifter.

How do I use the percentage table?

Pick a percentage that matches your goal and rep range, then use the weight shown as a starting point for your working sets.

Using the number

225 pounds for 5 reps is an Epley estimate of about 262 and a Brzycki estimate of about 253. The page reports the average, about 258. A training weight at 80% of that average is about 206 pounds, which the table labels as a 5 to 6 rep set. If you can already do 225 for 5, 206 for 5 should feel like a normal working set, not a test. If it does not, the estimate is high for you and you should train off the set you just did, not off the average.

Brzycki’s denominator is 37 minus reps. At 10 reps it is still sensible. Past the mid-30s the page stops using it and falls back toward the weight you lifted, which is a guardrail, not a real max. Neither formula knows whether the 5 reps were clean. A grindy fifth rep overestimates. The FAQ already says not to test a true one-rep max on your own. The percent table is the safer use of the number.

Sources & references

How to use it

  1. Measure the room's length, width and ceiling height. Measure floor to ceiling in the middle of the room, not in a corner where the drywall might be out of level.
  2. Count doors and windows. The calculator deducts 21 sq ft per door and 15 sq ft per window, which is a standard opening rather than your exact one.
  3. Choose whether the ceiling is part of the job. A ceiling adds length times width and usually changes the sheet length you want.
  4. Pick the sheet size you can actually buy and carry. A 4 × 12 sheet weighs about 70 pounds and will not fit in most cars.
  5. Read the sheet count first, then the materials list under it. The cost line is board only: mud, tape and screws are priced separately.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the ceiling. On a 12 × 14 room that is 168 sq ft, or about five extra sheets.
  • Deducting every opening at its exact size. A door casing covers more than the rough opening, and the offcut around a window is rarely reusable.
  • Buying 4 × 8 because it is cheaper per sheet. On a 12-foot wall, two 8-foot sheets create a butt joint that a 12-foot sheet does not.
  • Ordering mud by the gallon when it comes in a 4.5 gallon bucket. One bucket is the real minimum for a room.

Where the area comes from

The wall area is the room's perimeter times the ceiling height. For a 12 × 14 room with an 8 ft ceiling that is 52 ft around by 8 ft high, or 416 sq ft. Openings come off the top: 21 sq ft for a standard door, 15 sq ft for a standard window. With one door and two windows the walls are 365 sq ft.

Add the ceiling if it is part of the job, which is another 168 sq ft here, and the net area is 533 sq ft. Apply the waste allowance and you have the board to order. Every figure the page reports comes from that number, so if the sheet count looks wrong, check the area first.

The sheet count is the answer to a purchasing question, not a hanging plan. Board is priced per sheet and sold in the sizes the yard stocks, so a 4 × 12 sheet that covers a wall in one piece is often cheaper per square foot of wall than two 4 × 8 sheets plus the joint between them, even though the sheet costs more.

Finishing questions

How many screws do I need per sheet of drywall?

About 32 screws for a 4 × 8 sheet when studs are 16 inches apart and screws go every 12 inches: three rows of 8 down the 8-foot side. A 4 × 12 sheet takes about 48. Buy by weight, roughly one pound per 200 square feet, and keep a box of the next length up.

Can I hang drywall horizontally instead of vertically?

Yes, and for walls it is usually better. Horizontal sheets put the long factory edge at the top and bottom and spread joints away from the corners, which is where cracks start. It also means fewer vertical butt joints, which are the slowest ones to finish.

How much joint compound does a room use?

A ready-mixed estimate is about 4.5 gallons per 1,000 square feet of board for taping plus three coats. Small rooms almost always need one bucket regardless of the arithmetic, because you cannot buy half a bucket and you will want a second coat on the butt joints.

Should I add waste to the sheet count?

Yes. Ten percent is a sensible default for a plain rectangular room. Add more for a room with many openings, a diagonal or angled wall, or a ceiling full of recessed lights, because every cut-off piece has to be big enough to be useful.

Does this calculator work for ceilings and garages?

It does if you tell it the room dimensions and include the ceiling. Use a sheet long enough to span the ceiling in one direction where you can, and check local rules for garages: many areas require a fire-rated board between a garage and the house.

Where these rates come from

The coverage figures are the published planning numbers from the board manufacturers, not measurements of one room. Your mud use depends on how many coats you run and how tight your joints are.

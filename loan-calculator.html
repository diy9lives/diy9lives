How it works

The monthly payment uses the standard amortization formula: payment = amount × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n), where r is the monthly rate (APR ÷ 12) and n is the number of months. Total interest is the payment times n minus the amount borrowed. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between APR and interest rate?

The interest rate is the cost of borrowing the money. APR also folds in certain fees, so it is better for comparing offers. Enter the APR if you have it.

Why does a longer term cost more overall?

A longer term lowers the monthly payment, but you pay interest on the balance for more months, so total interest rises.

Does this include origination fees?

No. Some lenders charge an origination fee that is taken out of the loan proceeds, so you receive less than you borrow. Compare offers using APR, which accounts for it.

Can I pay the loan off early?

Usually yes, and doing so saves interest. Ask whether the lender charges a prepayment penalty before you sign.

Using the number

A $12,000 loan at 9.9% for 3 years is about $387 a month. You pay about $1,920 of interest. Stretching the same loan to 5 years drops the payment to about $254 and raises the interest to about $3,260. The lower payment costs about $1,340. That is the whole decision this page can make: whether the monthly relief is worth the extra interest, on a fixed rate with no fees.

If the lender quotes an APR of 11% and an interest rate of 9.9%, type 9.9 into the rate box. The APR is higher because it includes fees this formula never adds to the balance. A $300 origination fee is $300 you pay up front or that gets rolled in. Add it to the amount if it is financed. Do not expect the payment here to match a loan that starts interest-only or that penalizes you for paying it off early. For several cards at once, use the debt payoff planner. For a mortgage, use the mortgage page, which also asks for tax and insurance.

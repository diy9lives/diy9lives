How to read the score
📈
90–100: Very easy, understood by an average 11-year-old. 60–70: Plain English, easily understood by 13–15 year olds. 0–30: Very difficult, best understood by university graduates.
💬
Most web and marketing copy targets 60–70 — conversational and widely accessible. Academic and legal writing often scores much lower, which isn't necessarily bad, just denser.
⚠
Syllable counting is approximate (based on vowel-group patterns), so scores are a close estimate, not a certified linguistic measurement.

Paste the text and pick a measure

Paste a paragraph, a page, or a whole draft. The checker counts words and sentences, estimates syllables, and returns a Flesch Reading Ease score out of 100 with the grade level that goes with it. Higher is easier. Both inputs update as you type, so you can edit in place and watch the number move.

Check a representative section rather than the whole document if the document has very different parts. A readable introduction and an unreadable terms section average out to a score that describes neither.

Worked example: a 180-word page

Take a twenty-word paragraph split into two sentences, with about thirty syllables in total.

Now cut one long word. Dropping from 1.5 to 1.4 syllables per word raises the score by about 8 points, because the syllable term is weighted at 84.6 against the sentence-length term at 1.015. That weighting is the practical lesson: in this formula, word length costs you roughly eighty times more than sentence length per unit. Shortening sentences helps; choosing shorter words helps far more.

What the grade level means

Flesch Reading Ease was published by Rudolf Flesch in 1948 in the Journal of Applied Psychology, and the grade-level version comes from a 1975 study by J. Peter Kincaid and colleagues for the US Navy. The formula is 206.835 minus 1.015 times average sentence length, minus 84.6 times average syllables per word. Everything the score reports follows from those two averages.

Read the bands as a description of the text, not of the reader. A score in the sixties is plain English that most adults read without effort; a score in the thirties is legal drafting. Neither is automatically wrong. A safety notice should score high. A contract that scores high may be hiding an ambiguity, so simplify the sentence, not the meaning.

The syllable counter is an estimate. It strips common endings and counts vowel groups, which handles most English words and mangles some: abbreviations, product names, and words borrowed from other languages. Long numbers count as one syllable each, which flatters a page of figures. Treat a one or two point difference as noise and look at the trend.

Readability questions

What score should I aim for?

Sixty to seventy suits general web writing. Anything above eighty is very easy, which is right for instructions and wrong for a nuanced argument.

Why did my score drop after I added a source list?

Names, titles and citations are long and full of syllables. The formula sees a harder text; a reader skips that section. Score the body copy separately.

Does it count headings as sentences?

Headings without end punctuation run into the text around them, which inflates the average sentence length. Remove headings before pasting if you want a clean measure of the prose.

Is a low grade level insulting to readers?

No. Short sentences and plain words are how technical writers keep experts reading too. Complexity that does not carry meaning is just friction.

Which is better, this or a reading-time estimate?

They answer different questions. This tells you how hard the text is; the word counter tells you how long it takes to read. Use both.

Quick answers

What is the 28/36 rule?

A standard lending guideline: your monthly housing costs (mortgage, tax, insurance, HOA) shouldn't exceed 28% of your gross monthly income, and your total monthly debt payments (housing plus car loans, student loans, credit cards, etc.) shouldn't exceed 36%. It's a rule of thumb, not a law — some loan programs allow higher ratios.

Why does my down payment affect the home price I can afford?

A larger down payment means a smaller loan, which lowers your monthly principal and interest — freeing up more of your DTI budget for a higher purchase price. It also usually gets you a better interest rate and avoids PMI (private mortgage insurance) if you put down 20% or more.

Does this include closing costs?

No. This calculates ongoing monthly affordability (PITI) only. Closing costs typically run 2–5% of the loan amount and are usually paid upfront, separate from your down payment — budget for those separately.

How it works

The tool starts from your gross monthly income. The housing limit is the smaller of two numbers: the front-end share of income (28% in the conservative case) and the back-end share (36%) minus your existing monthly debt payments. It then works backward from that monthly budget through the mortgage payment formula, subtracting property tax, insurance, and HOA dues, to find the loan amount you can support, and adds your down payment to get a home price. The scenario table repeats this with looser ratios.

Using the number

Someone earning $90,000, with $400 a month of other debt payments, $40,000 to put down, a 6.5% rate, a 30-year term, property tax at 1.1% of the price, $1,500 a year of insurance, and no HOA, has a front-end cap of 28% of gross, about $2,100 a month. The back-end cap of 36% minus the $400 of other debt is about $2,300. The tighter one wins, so the housing budget is $2,100. Turned into a price, that is roughly a $308,000 house and a $268,000 loan.

The moderate and stretch rows on the page (31/43 and 43/50) will print higher prices from the same income. Those are not permission. A lender’s preapproval uses credit, reserves, and the actual tax bill, and it can be lower than every row here. If the other-debt box is missing a car payment or a student loan, the back ratio is wrong and the price is too high. Run the debt payoff planner on those balances before you treat the house number as real.

The useful decision is the gap between the conservative price and the one you like. If they are far apart, the fix is the rate, the down payment, or the other debt, not a hope that the tax bill stays flat.

Quick answers

What is the 28/36 rule?

A standard lending guideline: your monthly housing costs (mortgage, tax, insurance, HOA) shouldn't exceed 28% of your gross monthly income, and your total monthly debt payments (housing plus car loans, student loans, credit cards, etc.) shouldn't exceed 36%. It's a rule of thumb, not a law — some loan programs allow higher ratios.

Why does my down payment affect the home price I can afford?

A larger down payment means a smaller loan, which lowers your monthly principal and interest — freeing up more of your DTI budget for a higher purchase price. It also usually gets you a better interest rate and avoids PMI (private mortgage insurance) if you put down 20% or more.

Does this include closing costs?

No. This calculates ongoing monthly affordability (PITI) only. Closing costs typically run 2–5% of the loan amount and are usually paid upfront, separate from your down payment — budget for those separately.

Finding your ceiling, not your target

  1. Enter gross annual income and the monthly minimum payments on car loans, student loans and cards. Lenders count those before they count the house.
  2. Add the down payment, rate, term, property tax rate and insurance. The result is a price a lender might approve, not a price you should spend.
  3. Read the conservative, moderate and stretch scenarios. The difference between them is the debt-to-income cap, and it is the number that moves the answer.
  4. Compare the monthly payment with what you actually want to spend after childcare, savings and the costs of a larger home.

How to use this calculator

It answers one question: given what you earn and what you already owe, what price can you carry? The order of the inputs matters more than the precision of any one of them.

  1. Enter gross income, before tax and before deductions. Take-home pay is not the number lenders use, and it is not the number this page uses.
  2. Add every monthly debt payment that a credit report would show: car loans, student loans, minimum card payments, a personal loan. Rent is not a debt for this purpose, but a mortgage you are keeping is.
  3. Enter the down payment as cash, then the rate and term you have actually been quoted. A rate you hope for produces a price you cannot buy.
  4. Set the property tax rate from the county's assessment, not from a round number. It is the most commonly understated input here.
  5. Add insurance and HOA dues. Both reduce the loan you can support, because the budget has to cover them.

A worked example: $90,000 a year with a car payment

Gross income of $90,000 is $7,500 a month. The conservative front-end cap is 28%, or $2,100. The back-end cap is 36%, or $2,700, minus $400 of existing monthly debt, which leaves $2,300. The lower of the two wins, so the housing budget is $2,100.

From that budget, subtract property tax at 1.1% of the price and $1,500 a year of insurance, which is $125 a month. What remains services the loan. At 6.5% over 30 years that supports a loan of about $268,000, and adding the $40,000 down payment gives a price near $308,000. The principal and interest is $1,693, plus $282 of tax and $125 of insurance, and it lands back on $2,100.

Notice which number did the work. The $400 car payment cost roughly $40,000 of buying power, because every $400 no longer available each month removes the payment it could have supported for 360 months. The down payment added $40,000 of price, one for one.

What your results mean

The price is a ceiling, not a target. It is the most a lender's ratios say you can carry with nothing else changing, and living at the ceiling means every future raise is already spent before it arrives.

The 28/36 figures and the 43% back-end line are lending conventions, not law. A lender's preapproval also looks at credit history, reserves, employment stability, and the actual tax bill on the specific house. It can come in below every row on this page, and it can occasionally come in above.

The scenario rows repeat the same income with looser ratios. They are there so you can see how much price a ratio change buys, not because a looser ratio makes the payment affordable. Nothing in the math changes when the label changes.

What is not included: closing costs, moving costs, immediate repairs, and the fact that a first year of ownership usually costs more than the mortgage. Budget a separate cash reserve for those before you spend the down payment down to zero.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use gross or take-home income?

Gross. Lenders compare debt to gross income, so a take-home figure would understate your capacity by roughly the tax you pay. Keep the take-home number for your own budget check afterwards.

Is 28/36 a rule I have to follow?

No. It is a conservative convention. Many loans are approved with a back-end ratio near 43%, and some programs allow more. A higher ratio means less margin when a payment changes.

How much does a car payment reduce my price?

Roughly the loan that payment could support. At 6.5% for 30 years, every $100 a month of other debt removes about $15,800 of mortgage, so a $400 payment is about $63,000 of borrowing capacity. Paying the car off before house-hunting is often worth more than another year of saving.

What about a 15-year term?

The same monthly budget supports a much smaller loan, because each payment retires principal faster. The price falls, and the interest saved is real. Run both terms before deciding which house you are shopping for.

Do HOA dues count against me?

Yes. Lenders include them in the housing payment, and this page subtracts them from the same budget. A $350 HOA is $350 less available for principal and interest every month.

Where does the tax rate go wrong?

Assessment caps, homestead exemptions, and millage rates mean your neighbour's effective rate may not be yours. Look up the actual bill on a comparable house in the same county rather than using a state average.

What the affordability number cannot see

A lender's debt-to-income limit is not the same as a comfortable budget. This estimate does not know your childcare, medical costs, student loans in deferment, future income changes, or how much you want to keep saving. It also does not include closing costs or the cost of furnishing a larger home. Treat the top of the range as a ceiling, not a target.

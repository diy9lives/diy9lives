How it works

For a rectangle, volume in cubic feet is length × width × thickness in inches ÷ 12. For a round pad it is π × radius squared × thickness ÷ 12. The tool adds your extra percentage for spills and uneven ground, then divides by 27 to get cubic yards. Bag counts divide the cubic feet by the yield of each bag size: about 0.30 for a 40 lb bag, 0.375 for 50 lb, 0.45 for 60 lb, and 0.60 for 80 lb, rounded up.

Frequently asked questions

How thick should a concrete slab be?

Four inches is common for walkways, patios, and shed slabs. Driveways and heavier loads often use five to six inches. Check local building code, especially for footings.

Should I use bags or order a truck?

One cubic yard equals about 45 bags of 80 lb mix. For anything near a yard or more, ready-mix delivery is usually easier and often cheaper than mixing bags by hand.

Why add 10% extra?

Subgrade is rarely perfectly level and some concrete is lost to spills. Running short mid-pour can leave a weak seam, so ordering a little extra is cheap insurance.

Does this include gravel base or rebar?

No. It only calculates the concrete itself. Base material and reinforcement are separate and depend on your project and local requirements.

Using the number

A 10 by 12 foot patio, 4 inches thick, is 40 cubic feet before waste. Ten percent extra makes 44 cubic feet, which is 1.63 cubic yards. An 80 pound bag of ordinary concrete mix yields about 0.60 cubic feet, so you are looking at about 74 bags. That is a pallet and a bad afternoon. A ready-mix truck usually starts to make sense around a yard, and it is the obvious choice past two, once you include the base you still have to build.

The FAQ already says the page does not order gravel or rebar. Budget them anyway. A 4 inch slab on bare dirt is a cracked slab. The yields match QUIKRETE’s concrete-mix sheet: 0.30, 0.375, 0.45, and 0.60 cubic feet for the 40, 50, 60, and 80 pound bags. A different brand’s “high strength” bag can yield less. Read that bag, and do not order the exact yard. Short loads and overage fees are how a 1.63 yard pour becomes a more expensive number than the chart.
